# The chapter list, the sound and the look

The brief is signed off and the spine is picked, and both are in your brief. You write the
chapter list, which every chapter's beats are written from, and the sound and the look in words.

## Read

- `claims.json` in the plan folder, and the material the brief's `source` row names.
- [../references/sections.md](../references/sections.md) for the form of what you write.
- The brand's voice and look (`/workspace/brand/`), for the sound and the look.

## The chapter list

A short open, three to eight chapters, a short close. The parts add up to the length in the
brief, exactly.

- **The open** (15 to 30 s) asks the episode's question with the spine's real example on screen.
  It opens the brief's gap: by its end the viewer knows what is hard, on the example, in their
  own terms. It promises nothing, introduces nobody, and spends no seconds on credits or
  caveats: the source is a line on the picture.
- **A chapter** (45 to 100 s, 60 to 90 as a rule) has its own question (`asks`) and ends on a
  result the viewer can see (`ends`). It is one step of the explanation, and one thing done to
  the spine's example.
- **The close** (15 to 40 s) lands the idea with the spine's example in its final form. No card,
  no call to subscribe.

One question a chapter. If `asks` needs "and" to hold a second question, it is two chapters, or
one of the two is left out. The brief has a row for what the film leaves out: use it. A spare
chapter packed with what the others did not cover is a list, and the viewer hears it as one.

Each chapter earns its place by what the idea cannot be understood without. Say to yourself why
the film needs it. "The material also covers this" is not a reason: cut it.

The viewer hears the idea outright by the end of the first chapter, before the evidence for it:
every chapter after that is evidence. Try the list both ways before you report. Take out the
chapters that are mechanism and numbers: the open, the turn and the close must still say what
the film is about. Then take those three out: if what is left still reads as a whole film, it
is a tour of the material, not an explanation.

## The turn

Every film pivots once. Name where (`turn`), on the row of the one chapter that holds it: the
moment as the viewer sees it happen, in one sentence. In an explainer it is usually where the
obvious way breaks, or where the clever way shows its price: the ranked list comes back, and
the page that answers the question is not on it. A plan with no turn is a tour, one more thing
that works after another.

The turn is an image, not an abstraction: "word matching has limits" is not a turn; the list
with the right page missing is. The chapters before it build toward it, and the chapters after
it answer it.

## The first line and the last

Each chapter's words are written by a different person, so the two lines that frame the film are
yours. The team reads them at the sign-off, and the open and the close are held to them word for
word.

- `first`, on the open's row: the film's first spoken line. It is a hook: it opens the brief's
  gap, in words the viewer would use themselves, never the material's headings.
  `hook` names which kind it is, written exactly so: question (one they cannot answer yet),
  surprising number, counterintuitive claim, shared pain (a confusion they already feel),
  metaphor (the abstract thing made concrete), scenario ("Say you ask…") or stakes (what goes
  wrong without it). A line that introduces, credits or defines is none of these.
- `last`, on the close's row: the film's last spoken line, said over the opening picture in its
  final form. It lands the idea; it does not recite it. The brief's sentence was written for
  the team to read, and said again at the end it is a thesis statement where the film should
  close. `landing` names how the line lands, written exactly so: callback (it answers the first
  line, in the words the first line used), distillation (the idea cut down to a line the viewer
  could repeat tomorrow), generalization (what this one example shows about every case like
  it) or invitation (the one thing to try, or to watch for, next time). Not a recap, and not a
  goodbye.

Both are lines someone would say aloud, a whole sentence each: the first in 26 words at most,
the last in 16.

The chain is the film: each chapter's result must raise the next chapter's question. Read the
`ends` and `asks` down the list as one paragraph; where a question does not follow from the
result before it, the order is wrong or a chapter is missing.

Give each chapter the `shape` of its argument in a few words, and vary them: the naive version
and where it breaks; one diagram that grows; a race between two ways; a zoom from the whole to
one part; a before and after. Five chapters in one shape is a slideshow.

`covers` is the chapter's share of the material, in two or three sentences: which part of the
post it uses, which of the ledger's claims are its to make, and what it leaves to the chapters
beside it. Give it the reason the material gives, not only the result: a chapter that reports
numbers without the mechanism behind them is a summary, not an explanation. Each chapter's beats
are written by a different person, at the same time, with the material open: `covers` is what
keeps two of them from telling the same part. Say in one row's `covers` where the brief's idea
is first said outright (the first chapter, as a rule), and in a later one where it comes back
and pays off.

`handoff` says what crosses the cut into each part after the first, in one sentence: the
object, the state it leaves in, and where it arrives ("the question card stays at the top of the
frame, unchanged, and the two slices appear under it"). Every join is a match cut on that
object, so name it the same way on both sides. `cue` is the sound at the chapter's start.

Write all of it in words. No positions or sizes in pixels, no font names or sizes, no colour
values: the storyboard measures those from the frame. An `ends` is one sentence on what the
viewer sees, not a layout.

## The sound and the look

Four rows, in words: the voice (who, and the pace: it speaks at about 130 words a minute and
leaves room for the picture, so the film averages near 90); the music and how it moves with the
chapters; the look, from the brand; and the picture the episode returns to.

## Bar

- Every row can be read in half a minute and handed to someone who has the material but not your
  head: the question, the result, the shape, what it covers and the handoff say what its chapter
  must do.
- A rule that holds for the whole film (who to attribute to, what is illustrative) is said once,
  in the brief. Rows do not repeat it. The look row says where the picture carries its two
  standing lines, so every chapter draws them the same way: the source, and the brief's `label`
  for made-up material.
- A result is something on screen, not a summary: "the right page is at the top of the list",
  not "the viewer understands ranking".
- Read the `asks` down the list: one question each. Read the first line and the last together:
  the last answers or completes the first, and is not the brief's sentence again.

## Report

The chapters in a line. Then the JSON your brief asks for, whole.
