# The brief and its ledger

You write the two sections the team signs off before anything is planned: the brief, and the
ledger of what the film may state. Every later writer takes its facts from your ledger, and some
of them will never open the material, so what you leave out is left out of the film.

## Read

- The material, every file your brief lists, whole. Read it for how the thing works and why, not
  for its headings: an article is an information dump, and the film is a guided act of
  understanding.
- [../SKILL.md](../SKILL.md) for what an episode is, and [../references/sections.md](../references/sections.md)
  for the form of what you write.

## The brief

Seven rows at most, one or two sentences each. Label them as the team reads them: "The one idea",
"The gap it closes", "Who it is for", "Length", "Made from", "Made up is marked", "Leaves out".

- `idea`: one sentence the episode proves, in the viewer's words. It says how something works,
  not that it is good. If you need "and" to finish it, you have two episodes: pick the one the
  material proves best.
- `gap`: what the viewer cannot do or cannot see before the film, in words they would use: the
  confusion, the cost or the open question the idea resolves. It is why they would watch, and
  the film's first line opens it. Say it about their work, not about the material: "you cannot
  tell why search found a page with none of your words", not "the post explains embeddings". If
  you cannot name a gap, the idea is a description, not an explanation: find the question the
  material is really answering.
- `for`: who it is for, and what they already know. The second half decides what the film can
  skip.
- `length`: how long it runs and what leads it, with `seconds` as a round number between 240
  and 600. Size it by what the material can carry: a chapter is one step of the explanation and
  runs about a minute.
- `source`: what the material is, in one line a credit could use (its title, who wrote it, when).
  `names` lists what the voice would call whoever the facts come from ("Example Studio"), not the
  thing being explained; leave it empty when the material is the team's own. `files` lists the
  material's paths, as your brief gave them.
- `label`: the one word the picture carries wherever something is made up ("Illustrative").
  Write it when the ledger marks anything illustrative; every chapter then draws that word and
  no other, and the voice never spends a line on it.
- `leaves`: what the material covers that this episode leaves out, in a sentence, so the team
  can disagree before the plan is written.

## The ledger

One row per thing the film may state as a fact: eight to twenty rows. A reader should be able to
check any row against the material in a minute.

- `claim`: the fact as the film will state it, in one plain sentence.
- `quote`: the material's own words for it, word for word, so it can be found there. Cut a long
  passage with … and keep the part that carries the fact.
- `status`: `stated` (the material says it, and the quote shows where), `illustrative` (made up
  to show the idea: names, counts, a sample answer) or `unconfirmed` (the film would need it and
  the material does not say it).

Write the reasons, not only the results. "It scored 90% better" is a result; the row the film
needs most is the one beside it that says why. Give every number its own row, with the unit and
what it was measured against. What the material does not say stays unsaid: a number it did not
give is left out, or marked illustrative and never passed off as real.

Keep the material's concrete cases. Where it grounds a point in an instance, a comparison or a
worked figure, that instance gets a row of its own: "a simple lookup gets one helper and a
handful of calls" lands, and "effort scales with complexity" does not. A chapter can only make a
thing concrete with what the ledger holds.

Where the material names an example without its details (a query with no published answer), add
an illustrative row that says how the details will be shown: a few plainly fictional names. A
screen of the word "placeholder" explains nothing.

## Bar

- The idea stands alone: someone who reads only that sentence knows what the film is about.
- The gap is the viewer's, and the idea closes it: read the two rows together as a question and
  its answer.
- Every `stated` row's quote is in the material, word for word. It is checked.
- Nothing about the channel, the brand or the viewer subscribing.

## Report

The idea in a line. Then the JSON your brief asks for, whole.
